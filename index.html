🏠 Climate risk for homebuyers

"It's only a 100-year flood zone." Should you still buy the house?

A flood map says a 1% chance each year. That sounds like nothing. Over a 30-year mortgage it isn't, and with a warming climate, 1% won't stay 1%. Play with the sliders, watch the water rise, and live through 30 years of rolling the dice.

Quick guess 🎲

A house has a 1% chance of flooding every year. What's the chance it floods at least once during a 30-year mortgage?

The decision

You're a first-time homebuyer who found a house you like. The listing says it's in FEMA's "100-year floodplain" (a Special Flood Hazard Area). You need to decide: Do you buy it? If you do, is flood insurance worth the yearly premium? (With a federally backed mortgage, the lender will require it inside this zone. Just outside the zone, it's optional, and most people skip it.)

The concept: return periods don't mean what people think

A "100-year flood" doesn't come once every 100 years on a schedule. It's a flood with a 1-in-100 (1%) chance of happening in any single year. Each year you own the house is another roll of the dice, so the chance of getting hit at least once keeps growing:

Chance of at least one flood in N years = 1 − (1 − p)N

Flood maps assume p stays fixed. But sea-level rise and heavier downpours (warmer air holds about 7% more water vapor per °C) mean the odds are going up. The IPCC projects that at most coastal locations, sea levels that used to happen once a century will happen at least once a year sometime this century. So a 1% flood today might be a 2–3% flood by the time you've paid off the house.

Sanity check (do it on a calculator)

With a 1% chance, 0% growth, and 30 years: chance of no flood in one year = 0.99. No flood for 30 years in a row = 0.9930 ≈ 0.740. So the chance of at least one flood = 1 − 0.740 = 0.260 → 26%. The page should show exactly that. Expected damage = 30 × 0.01 × $65,000 = $19,500, and the break-even premium = $19,500 ÷ 30 = $650/yr.

How the math works

  • Flood chance in year t: pt = p₀ × (1 + g)t−1, capped at 100%. Here p₀ is today's chance and g is the yearly growth rate.
  • Chance of at least one flood by year N: 1 − (1−p₁)(1−p₂)…(1−pN). Each year is treated as independent. With g = 0 this simplifies to 1 − (1−p₀)N.
  • Equivalent return period: 1 / pt. A 2% chance acts like a "50-year flood."
  • Expected number of floods: p₁ + p₂ + … + pN. Expected damage = expected floods × cost per flood.
  • Break-even ("actuarially fair") premium: expected damage ÷ years owned. Real premiums run higher because of insurer costs and the fact that losses are correlated (one storm floods thousands of homes at once).
  • Dice-roll simulator and neighbors: each year a random number between 0 and 1 is drawn. If it's below pt, that year floods. These use the exact same pt as the chart, so over many runs they average out to the chart's numbers.
  • Buy or walk away: extra flood cost = (floodplain premiums × years) − (expected damage at the 0.2% house). If the price discount is bigger than that, the floodplain house wins on average.
  • Where the ~1%/yr "inland" setting comes from: the Fifth National Climate Assessment finds that heavy rainstorms have become more frequent and more intense across most of the U.S., with the biggest increases for the rarest events. The amount of rain falling on the heaviest 1% of days rose about 45% in the Midwest and 60% in the Northeast from 1958 to 2021. Spread over 63 years, a 45% rise works out to about 0.6% per year (1.451/63 ≈ 1.006). Since rare events are rising faster, we round to ~1% per year as an illustrative, deliberately modest inland rate (much slower than the coastal case). The physical reason is the Clausius–Clapeyron rule from class: warmer air holds about 7% more water vapor per °C.
  • Where the ~6%/yr "fast" setting comes from: if a 1% event becomes a yearly (~100%) event in ~75 years, that's 100× more often, and 1001/75 ≈ 1.063, so about 6.3% growth per year.

Limitations

This is a teaching model, not a quote. It assumes steady exponential growth in flood odds (real trends depend on location, emissions, and local flood defenses). It treats years as independent and gives every flood the same cost. It also ignores deductibles, coverage limits (NFIP caps building coverage at $250,000), inflation, and premium changes under FEMA's Risk Rating 2.0 (which can raise a policy up to 18% per year until it reaches the full-risk rate). In the simulator, neighbors are independent, but in real life one storm floods a whole street at once.
